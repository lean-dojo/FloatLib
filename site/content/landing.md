When you write `x + y` in a FloatLib program, you run the same operation the theorem talks about. [`ExecFloat.Proof.add_eq_spec`](#/chapter/using-the-library/proving-arithmetic-agrees-with-its-specification) says the executable addition equals the format's reference addition, whichever backend the planner picked, so the rounding is the same and so is the meaning of every result bit.

- **Your formats and rounding rules.** You can [define your own format](#/chapter/using-the-library/choosing-a-format) in the same framework. Say you want a binary layout nobody ships yet: you pick the exponent and fraction widths, the bias, and how zeros, infinities, and NaNs are encoded. A new representation or rounding rule goes through the same shared interfaces, and Lean proofs connect its executable operations to their numerical specification.
- **The classical results, connected to code.** Correct rounding, half-ulp error bounds, and Sterbenz's lemma for exact subtraction are part of the [rounding theory](#/chapter/the-mathematics-of-rounding). Each comes with its hypotheses, such as finite inputs and no overflow, and when your program meets them you can use the result on its arithmetic.
- **Numerical bounds that close proofs.** The [`interval` tactic](#/chapter/proving-numerical-bounds) proves real inequalities from rational bounds on the inputs. It handles elementary functions and subdivides adaptively, and its evaluator can work with exact rational endpoints, an integer binary grid, or a custom endpoint format, all through the same containment contract.
- **NaNs, infinities, and both zeros are in the proofs.** The [IEEE model](#/chapter/ieee-binary-formats) spells out their encodings and behavior, down to comparisons and exception flags, so a theorem about the complete result word can tell apart things that equality over the reals can't.
- **Many formats, shared foundations.** IEEE binary and decimal, [small ML formats](#/chapter/low-precision-formats-for-machine-learning), [posits](#/chapter/posits-and-the-quire), and [P3109](#/chapter/p3109) sit next to fixed point, logarithmic, codebook, and block-scaled representations. Posits also get exact quire accumulation, up to the quire's capacity, and real-rounding proofs for roots, powers, exponentials, and logarithms.

Speed is a big part of the work for us. We want to get close to MPFR's speed while keeping the connection between the arithmetic and its proofs. The [backend kernels](#/chapter/backends-and-the-planner) use small lookup tables, machine-word algorithms, and limb arithmetic for wider values, and the planner picks an implementation for each operation. Every certified choice has a proof that it agrees with the same specification, and since Lean erases proof terms when it compiles, the arithmetic never re-checks its theorem at runtime [@leanReference]. The [performance chapter](#/chapter/performance) has the measurements from 2 to 4,096 bits, and it shows where we're still behind.

We also check FloatLib against other people's implementations, a lot. Against SoftFloat and MPFR we ran all 154 billion binary16 and bfloat16 operation pairs and binary32 square roots, and the values and all five flags agreed in the four rounding directions IEEE 754 requires for binary formats. In the wider IEEE formats, FloatLib matched Berkeley TestFloat on more than 102 million cases. We compare against MPFR, posit libraries, published small-format tables, and MPFX too. The binary elementary functions match MPFR on every case in our elementary-function test corpus, and the MPFX comparisons found four bugs, which we reported upstream. There are some subtle differences with SoftPosit, which we [explain in the posits chapter](#/chapter/posits-and-the-quire/cross-checks-against-universal-and-softposit), and the [external validation chapter](#/chapter/external-validation) gives all the inputs and results.

If you want to add a format, improve a proof, or explain something better, the [contributing guide](https://github.com/lean-dojo/FloatLib/blob/main/CONTRIBUTING.md) is the place to start. We'd love to see what you build with FloatLib :)
