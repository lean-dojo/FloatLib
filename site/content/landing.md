In binary32, `0.1 + 0.2` prints as `0.3`. The number actually stored is `5033165 / 16777216`, a hair above three tenths, which binary32 can't hold exactly. FloatLib lets you compute that sum, ask for its exact value and prove things about it in one Lean file, and the theorems are about the very code that ran. When you write `x + y`, [`ExecFloat.Proof.add_eq_spec`](#/chapter/using-the-library/how-to-prove-your-first-theorem) says the executable addition equals the format's reference addition, whichever backend the planner picked. Same rounding, same meaning for every result bit.

- **Bring your own format.** Want a binary layout nobody ships? [Define your own format](#/chapter/using-the-library/which-formats-can-you-use): pick the exponent and fraction widths, the bias, and how zeros, infinities and NaNs are encoded. A new representation or rounding rule goes through the same shared interfaces, and Lean proofs connect its executable operations to their numerical specification.
- **The textbook theorems, on your arithmetic.** Correct rounding, half-ulp error bounds and Sterbenz's lemma for exact subtraction are all in the [rounding theory](#/chapter/the-mathematics-of-rounding). Each one carries its hypotheses, such as finite inputs and no overflow, and when your program meets them you can apply the result to the arithmetic that actually ran.
- **Tactics that close numerical goals.** Hand the [`interval` tactic](#/chapter/proving-numerical-bounds) rational bounds on the inputs and it proves the real inequality, elementary functions included, subdividing adaptively. Its evaluator runs on exact rational endpoints, an integer binary grid or a custom endpoint format, all under the same containment contract. Point the [`roundoff` tactic](#/chapter/proving-numerical-bounds/how-to-get-an-error-bound-for-floating-point-code) at a configured binary program and it works from the code to an error bound, stating the range conditions that bound needs.
- **The awkward values are in the proofs too.** NaNs, infinities and both zeros have their encodings and behavior spelled out in the [IEEE model](#/chapter/ieee-binary-formats), down to comparisons and exception flags. Positive and negative zero are the same real number, but dividing one by each gives $+\infty$ and $-\infty$, and a theorem about the complete result word keeps them apart where equality over the reals can't.
- **Lots of formats, shared foundations.** IEEE binary and decimal, [small ML formats](#/chapter/low-precision-formats-for-machine-learning), [posits](#/chapter/posits-and-the-quire) and [P3109](#/chapter/low-precision-formats-for-machine-learning/what-is-ieee-p3109) live alongside fixed point, logarithmic, codebook and block-scaled representations. Posits get two extras. Their quire accumulates exactly, up to its capacity, and roots, powers, exponentials and logarithms come with proofs that they round the real result correctly.

We care a lot about speed, because we want MPFR's speed without losing the connection between the arithmetic and its proofs. For each operation the planner picks an implementation from the [backend kernels](#/chapter/performance): small lookup tables, machine-word algorithms, and limb arithmetic for wider values. Every certified choice has a proof that it agrees with the same specification. Lean erases proof terms when it compiles, so the arithmetic never re-checks its theorem at runtime [@leanReference]. On ordinary binary64 calls our proved addition, subtraction and multiplication beat MPFR at the same precision. Our [performance chapter](#/chapter/performance) has the measurements from 2 to 4,096 bits, including the places where MPFR wins.

A proof only shows that the code matches the specification we wrote, so we also test FloatLib against other people's implementations, a lot. Against SoftFloat and MPFR we ran all 154 billion binary16 and bfloat16 operation pairs and binary32 square roots, and every value and all five flags agreed, in each of the four rounding directions IEEE 754 requires for binary formats. In the wider IEEE formats, FloatLib matched Berkeley TestFloat on more than 102 million cases. Other comparisons cover MPFR, posit libraries, published small-format tables and MPFX. On every case in our elementary-function test corpus the binary elementary functions match MPFR, and the MPFX comparisons turned up four bugs in MPFX, which we reported upstream. SoftPosit and FloatLib differ in a few subtle places, which we [explain in the posits chapter](#/chapter/posits-and-the-quire/how-we-compare-posits-with-universal-and-softposit), and the [external validation chapter](#/chapter/external-validation) gives all the inputs and results.
