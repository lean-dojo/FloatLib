The FP8 and FP4 formats of [chapter 11](#/chapter/low-precision-formats-for-machine-learning) assign their zero, infinity and NaN codes differently. The P3109 interim report describes a family through four parameters: total width $K$, precision $P$, signedness, and domain. A finite domain has no infinities, and an extended domain includes them. Together the parameters determine what each stored code means and how an exact input is projected into the format.

In P3109's Binary8p4se format, conversion with nearest-even rounding and no saturation request sends 232 to 224 and 233 to positive infinity. The precision-rounding step chooses between 224 and 240: 232 is a tie and rounds to the even choice, 224, while 233 rounds to 240. But 240 has no finite encoding in this format. The range rule turns it into infinity. P3109 keeps those steps separate: round to precision, apply the saturation policy, then encode. Following this rule and bounding the numerical error are separate proof obligations.

The report calls a representable value a *datum*, and its required instances are Binary8p4se, Binary8p3se, Binary4p2sf, and Binary8p1uf for block scales. FloatLib implements the parameterized family and proves its decoding, projection, and arithmetic rules.

We follow version 4.0.3 of the *IEEE Working Group P3109 Interim Report on Arithmetic Formats for Machine Learning* [@p3109InterimReport], which is a working-group report and hasn't been approved as an IEEE standard. Section numbers refer to that report. Sections 3.1 and 4.7.2 cover decoding, and 4.7.3 through 4.7.6 cover projection.

FLoPS, by Tung-Che Chang, Sehyeok Park, Jay P. Lim, and Santosh Nagarakatte, formalizes P3109 in Lean and studies how saturation and one-bit precision change familiar numerical algorithms [@flopsPaper]. Its FMCAD artifact also has executable bit-level arithmetic, with refinement proofs for rounding, saturation, projection and core operations [@flopsArtifact]. It is the closest related work to this part of FloatLib, and in chapter 19 we compare the [two developments and their proof coverage](#/chapter/external-validation/other-verified-numerical-libraries).

## Width, precision, signedness, and domain

In FloatLib a P3109 format is a value of [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format]] with four parameters. `bitWidth` is the report's $K$, and `precision` is its $P$, which counts the implicit leading bit, so only $P - 1$ bits are stored. Then there's a `Signedness` and a `Domain`. The descriptor also carries three proofs: $K > 2$, $P > 0$, and $P < K$ for signed formats but $P \le K$ for unsigned ones. Because the proofs live in the descriptor, the decoder and the projection kernel can use those inequalities wherever they need them. For literal parameters [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format.signed]] and `unsigned` prove them with `decide`, and `ofParameters?` checks them at run time.

Everything else comes from $K$ and $P$. There are $P - 1$ stored trailing bits, given by `trailingBits`. In a signed format the exponent field, `exponentBits`, has $K - P$ bits. In an unsigned one it has $K - P + 1$, because the bit that would have held the sign goes to the exponent instead. The bias, `exponentBias`, is a power of two, $B = 2^{w_e - 1}$ for exponent width $w_e$, so `exponentBias_signed` gives $B = 2^{K-P-1}$ and `exponentBias_unsigned` gives $B = 2^{K-P}$. That's one more than the IEEE bias $2^{w_e - 1} - 1$ for the same field width, and this one unit explains most of the differences from the OCP formats. `minimumNormalExponent` gives the smallest normal exponent, $1 - B$. In the report's terms, the spacing between adjacent values at a given exponent is the quantum, one unit in the last place. Across the whole format the smallest quantum is $2^{2 - P - B}$, and `minimumQuantumExponent` names its exponent $2 - P - B$. The descriptor we'll use most is [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format.binary8p4se]], named after the report's shorthand from Section 3.2, which reads as eight bits, precision four, signed, extended. `binary8p3se` is its precision-three sibling, and the examples below also use small descriptors that expose the unsigned and precision-one cases.

```lean
open FloatLib
open FloatLib.Floats
open FloatLib.Floats.Formats
open FloatLib.Numerics
open FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format (binary8p3se binary8p4se)

/-- The same layout with a finite datum set: no infinities. -/
def b8p4sf : P3109.Format := .signed 8 4 .finite

/-- Eight unsigned bits with precision eight: one exponent bit and no sign. -/
def b8p8uf : P3109.Format := .unsigned 8 8 .finite

#eval (binary8p4se.exponentBits, binary8p4se.trailingBits, binary8p4se.exponentBias)
-- (4, 3, 8)

#eval (binary8p4se.minimumNormalExponent, binary8p4se.minimumQuantumExponent)
-- (-7, -10)

#eval (b8p8uf.exponentBits, b8p8uf.trailingBits, b8p8uf.exponentBias)
-- (1, 7, 1)

#eval (P3109.Format.ofParameters? 8 8 .signed .finite).isSome
-- false

#eval (P3109.Format.ofParameters? 8 8 .unsigned .finite).isSome
-- true
```

With precision four, the signed eight-bit format has four exponent bits and bias 8, so its normal exponents run from $-7$ to $7$. An unsigned format with $P = K$ is legal and has a single exponent bit. A signed format with $P = K$ would have no exponent field at all, and the checked constructor refuses it. Our binary-interchange `FloatFormat` descriptor needs at least two exponent bits and one fraction bit. P3109 allows one exponent bit and, at $P = 1$, no trailing bits, and its own descriptor admits those layouts.

## Zero, NaN, and infinity codes

Every P3109 format has a single NaN and a single zero, and depending on signedness and domain it has two infinities, one, or none. In a signed format the top bit splits the code space at `signBoundary`, $2^{K-1}$. Codes below it are positive, and codes above it are the same magnitudes negated. The boundary itself, the code that would be negative zero, is NaN. In an unsigned format NaN is the last code, $2^K - 1$, and in both cases the name is `nanBits`. In an extended domain, positive infinity, `positiveInfinityBits`, takes the code just below NaN. In a signed format that's $2^{K-1} - 1$, the last code of the positive half, and in an unsigned one it's $2^K - 2$. A signed extended format puts negative infinity, `negativeInfinityBits`, at the top code. So infinities sit at the ends of the finite ranges instead of taking up a whole exponent class, and no format in the family has a negative zero. [Figure 12.1](#/chapter/p3109/figure-ch15-code-space) shows the full `binary8p4se` code strip with those boundaries marked. Three comparison cards show how the boundaries move for the other two descriptors above and for the six-bit, precision-one descriptor used for the tie rule.

![Complete 256-code binary8p4se strip with a class key, plus comparisons for b8p4sf, b8p8uf, and b6p1sf; the precision-one format has no subnormals and its nonzero finite values are signed powers of two](assets/ch15-code-space.png "One complete P3109 code space, with three comparisons of reserved codes and finite values.")

For a positive finite code with biased exponent field $e$ and trailing field $t$, with $T = P - 1$ trailing bits, `decodePositiveFinite` computes

$$
x = \begin{cases}
t \cdot 2^{\,2 - P - B} & e = 0, \\[4pt]
(2^{T} + t) \cdot 2^{\,e - B + 1 - P} & e \ge 1,
\end{cases}
$$

The second row is a normal number, where the implicit leading one contributes $2^T$, the stored bits fill in $t$, and the exponent scales that $P$-bit integer. The first row is the subnormals of [chapter 02](#/chapter/from-reals-to-machine-numbers), which have no implicit one and a fixed scale, so they form a uniform grid down to zero. `decodeNat` wraps this formula in the reserved-code test, which is different for each of the four combinations of signedness and domain. On top of that, [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format.decode]] restricts the input to a `BitVec` of the descriptor's width and returns a `NumericalValue Dyadic`, which is an exact dyadic, a signed infinity, or NaN. Let's decode a few codes.

```lean
#eval (binary8p4se.nanBits, binary8p4se.positiveInfinityBits, binary8p4se.negativeInfinityBits)
-- (128, 127, 255)

#eval binary8p4se.decode 0x01
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := false, significand := 1, exponent := -10 }

#eval binary8p4se.decode 0x08
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := false, significand := 8, exponent := -10 }

#eval binary8p4se.decode 0x7e
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := false, significand := 14, exponent := 4 }

#eval binary8p4se.decode 0x7f
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.infinity false

#eval binary8p4se.decode 0x80
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.exceptional (FloatLib.Numerics.ExceptionalValue.nan none false false)

#eval binary8p4se.decode 0x81
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := true, significand := 1, exponent := -10 }

#eval binary8p4se.decode 0xff
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.infinity true
```

Code 1 is the least positive subnormal, $2^{-10}$, and code 8 is the least normal, $8 \cdot 2^{-10} = 2^{-7}$. Code 126 is the largest finite value, $14 \cdot 2^4 = 224$, because infinity has taken code 127. In a finite-domain format, code 127 is free for a finite value instead.

```lean
#eval (b8p4sf.nanBits, b8p4sf.maxFiniteBits)
-- (128, 127)

#eval b8p4sf.decode 0x7f
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := false, significand := 15, exponent := 4 }

#eval (binary8p4se.maxFinite.toRat, b8p4sf.maxFinite.toRat)
-- (224, 240)
```

`maxFiniteBits` is the largest finite code, its value is `maxFinite`, and `minFinite` is the negation, or zero for an unsigned format. With $P = K$, the unsigned format behaves like fixed point. With one exponent bit and bias 1, codes 0 through 254 form the uniform grid $c / 128$, and code 255 is NaN. No separate fixed-point case is needed in the decoder.

```lean
#eval (b8p8uf.nanBits, b8p8uf.maxFiniteBits)
-- (255, 254)

#eval b8p8uf.decode 0x7f
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := false, significand := 127, exponent := -7 }

#eval b8p8uf.decode 0x80
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := false, significand := 128, exponent := -7 }

#eval b8p8uf.maxFinite.toRat
-- 127 / 64
```

We prove these readings for every valid descriptor. `decode_zero`, [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format.decode_nanBits]], `decode_positiveInfinityBits` under the extended hypothesis, and `decode_negativeInfinityBits` under signed and extended give the named codes the meanings the report specifies. Ordering comes from [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format.decodePositiveFinite_strictMono]], which says that a larger positive finite code always has a larger value, across the subnormal boundary and including $P = 1$. Encoding needs that, because monotonicity is what proves that a value within the finite range never lands on a reserved code.

## Projection: round, then saturate, then encode

Conversion takes three steps, in the order the report specifies. First the exact input is rounded to the descriptor's precision. Then the rounded value is compared with the finite endpoints and the saturation rule applies, and finally the resulting datum is encoded. `roundToPrecision`, `saturate`, and the encoder behind `projectCode` do those three steps. [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format.projectValue]] names the result before encoding, so a proof can tell the projected datum apart from its stored code.

A policy is a [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.ProjectionPolicy]], which pairs a [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.RoundingMode]] with a [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.SaturationMode]]. Section 4.7.4 has nine rounding modes: toward zero, toward positive, toward negative, nearest with ties away from zero, nearest with ties to even, to odd, and three stochastic modes A, B, and C. Five of them also exist in IEEE 754 and keep the names of the binary modes, `towardZero`, `up`, `down`, `nearestAway`, and `nearest`, and the others are `toOdd`, `stochasticA`, `stochasticB`, and `stochasticC`. Each stochastic mode carries a `RandomBits` value, an exact-width `BitVec` of the $N$ random bits the report calls for. So the operation stays a pure function, and you can't pass an integer too wide for $N$ bits. Section 4.7.5 gives three saturation modes: finite, propagate, and none. The report names no default policy, but Section 4.5 requires its minimum operation specializations under the pair (NearestTiesToEven, SatNone) [@p3109InterimReport]. We made that pair the default policy `{}`, and it's also the default of the `mode` and `saturation` options that operations on P3109 values take. With a policy in hand, the rounding step, [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format.roundFiniteToPrecision]], starts by choosing the quantum exponent $Q$, so that the result will be an integer multiple of $2^Q$. For a nonzero input $x$ it is

$$
Q = \max\bigl(\lfloor \log_2 |x| \rfloor,\; 1 - B\bigr) - P + 1,
$$

and one unit in the last place of the result is $2^{Q}$. Taking the `max` lets the same formula cover subnormals, and `quantumExponent` names this value for the theorems. Then the input is written as $\pm(\ell + r/2^d) \cdot 2^Q$, where $d \ge 0$ is the number of input bits below the quantum, $\ell$ is an integer, and $0 \le r < 2^d$. Which of $\ell$ and $\ell + 1$ comes back is up to the mode. Nearest-even needs a different tie rule at $P = 1$. With a single significand bit every nonzero significand is $1$, so Section 4.7.4 tests the parity of the code instead. For a nonzero candidate that means asking whether $Q + B$ is even, because at $P = 1$ the code of $2^Q$ is its biased exponent $Q + B$. Zero counts as even. This test is `lowerCodeIsEven`, and for $P > 1$ it comes down to ordinary significand parity. Before we get to precision one, take an ordinary tie, say 17.

```lean
def seventeen : Numerics.Dyadic := { negative := false, significand := 17, exponent := 0 }

#eval binary8p4se.quantumExponent seventeen
-- 1

#eval binary8p4se.decode (binary8p4se.projectCode {} (.finite seventeen))
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := false, significand := 8, exponent := 1 }

#eval binary8p4se.decode (binary8p4se.projectCode { rounding := .nearestAway } (.finite seventeen))
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := false, significand := 9, exponent := 1 }

#eval binary8p4se.decode (binary8p4se.projectCode { rounding := .toOdd } (.finite seventeen))
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := false, significand := 9, exponent := 1 }
```

With four bits of precision the grid around 17 has spacing 2, so 17 is a tie between 16 and 18. Ties to even picks 16 because its significand 8 is even, while ties away picks 18, and so does round-to-odd, because 9 is odd. To see the $P = 1$ case we need a smaller format.

```lean
def b6p1sf : P3109.Format := .signed 6 1 .finite

/-- Exactly halfway between the codes 1 and 2 of `b6p1sf`, which denote 2^-15 and 2^-14. -/
def tie : Numerics.Dyadic := { negative := false, significand := 3, exponent := -16 }

#eval (b6p1sf.projectCode {} (.finite tie)).toNat
-- 2

#eval (b6p1sf.projectCode { rounding := .toOdd } (.finite tie)).toNat
-- 1

#eval [0, 1, 2, 3].map fun r =>
  (b6p1sf.projectCode { rounding := .stochasticA (.ofNat 2 r) } (.finite tie)).toNat
-- [1, 1, 2, 2]
```

Here $Q = -15$ and $B = 16$, so $Q + B$ is odd, which means the lower code 1 is odd. Nearest-even rounds up to code 2, while round-to-odd keeps code 1. Each stochastic mode turns the discarded fraction $r / 2^d$ into an $N$-bit integer and compares it with the supplied random bits. With the fraction at exactly one half and two random bits, half of the four draws round up, which is what an unbiased rule should do at a midpoint. Modes A, B and C differ in how they scale the fraction, and you can read the difference in `Internal.roundAway` in the [projection kernel](https://github.com/lean-dojo/FloatLib/blob/main/FloatLib/Floats/Formats/P3109/Projection/Runtime.lean). At precision one, a carry changes the exponent and leaves the normalized significand equal to one. Before rounding, the magnitude is $\tfrac32\cdot2^{-15}$. Rounding its coefficient up gives $2\cdot2^{-15}$, but a precision-one datum stores that as $1\cdot2^{-14}$, so the carry turns into an exponent increment. Both neighbours have normalized significand 1. Their stored exponent codes, 1 and 2, keep the parity distinction that the significands have lost. That's also why, in the code-space figure, the positive finite codes of `b6p1sf` denote successive powers of two with no trailing field between them.

## Saturation and overflow

After rounding, the value is compared with `minFinite` and `maxFinite`. Inside that interval nothing changes, and outside it the saturation mode and the rounding mode decide together what happens. `none` is the mode in our default policy and in the report's Section 4.5 specification. Under it, a finite value that rounds above the largest finite datum becomes positive infinity in an extended format and NaN in a finite one. As in IEEE 754, rounding toward zero or toward negative returns the largest finite value instead. An infinite input stays infinite if the format has an infinity and becomes NaN if it doesn't.

Under `finite`, every out-of-range value, finite or infinite, becomes the nearest finite endpoint. Under `propagate`, finite overflow is clamped as under `finite`, but an incoming infinity survives when the format has one. Let's push three hundred through a few of these policies.

```lean
def exact300 : Numerics.Dyadic := { negative := false, significand := 300, exponent := 0 }

#eval (binary8p4se.projectCode {} (.finite exact300)).toNat
-- 127

#eval (binary8p4se.projectCode { saturation := .finite } (.finite exact300)).toNat
-- 126

#eval (binary8p4se.projectCode { rounding := .towardZero } (.finite exact300)).toNat
-- 126

#eval (b8p4sf.projectCode {} (.finite exact300)).toNat
-- 128

#eval (b8p4sf.projectCode { saturation := .finite } (.finite exact300)).toNat
-- 127
```

Three hundred exceeds 224, the largest finite value of `binary8p4se`, so under the default policy it becomes code 127, positive infinity. Finite saturation clamps it to code 126 and rounding toward zero also returns 126. `b8p4sf` has no infinity, so there the default policy gives code 128, the NaN, and finite saturation gives the largest finite code 127.

Rounding comes before the range test, so a value slightly above `maxFinite` can round back down to it. Section 4.7.3 makes this point in its first note, and the conversion status follows it. For 232 and 233, we can now follow that order through the rounding and range tests.

```lean
def exact232 : Numerics.Dyadic := { negative := false, significand := 232, exponent := 0 }
def exact233 : Numerics.Dyadic := { negative := false, significand := 233, exponent := 0 }

#eval (binary8p4se.projectCode {} (.finite exact232)).toNat
-- 126

#eval (binary8p4se.projectCode {} (.finite exact233)).toNat
-- 127
```

In the grid row containing 224 the spacing is 16, so 232 is a tie between 224 and 240. The significand of 224 is 14, which is even, so nearest-even rounds to 224 and nothing overflows. At 233 the nearest grid point is 240, which isn't a datum of `binary8p4se`, and saturation turns it into infinity. Both inputs have leading exponent 7, so the quantum formula gives $Q=7-4+1=4$, and from there the precision step and the range step do different work.

| Input | Input in units of $2^4$ | Rounded coefficient | Rounded value | Default projected code |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 232 | $14+\tfrac12$ | 14, even | 224 | `0x7e`, finite |
| 233 | $14+\tfrac9{16}$ | 15 | 240 | `0x7f`, infinity |

Both rounded coefficients fit in four bits, and what goes wrong for 240 is the encoding. Its biased exponent would be 15 and its trailing field 7, which gives `0 1111 111`, the code reserved for infinity. Its neighbour 224 uses the same exponent with trailing field 6, `0 1111 110`, and is finite. Change the domain to finite and that first combination becomes available, so 240 becomes representable, and the precision rounding doesn't change at all. The projection proof keeps `FitsPrecisionGrid` separate from the finite range test: a value can fit the precision grid and still have no finite datum in the descriptor. The encoder's in-range hypothesis is there to rule out that collision with a reserved code. Infinite inputs to a finite-domain format put the three saturation modes side by side, so let's send positive infinity to the finite-domain sibling.

```lean
#eval (b8p4sf.projectCode {} (.infinity false)).toNat
-- 128

#eval (b8p4sf.projectCode { saturation := .propagate } (.infinity false)).toNat
-- 127

#eval (b8p4sf.projectCode { saturation := .finite } (.infinity false)).toNat
-- 127
```

In a format without infinity, `propagate` clamps just as `finite` does, and only `none` produces the NaN. Unsigned formats have a lower endpoint of zero, and the same rules apply there. A negative input becomes NaN under the default policy, and zero under finite saturation or a directed mode that rounds toward it.

```lean
def minusOne : Numerics.Dyadic := { negative := true, significand := 1, exponent := 0 }

#eval (b8p8uf.projectCode {} (.finite minusOne)).toNat
-- 255

#eval (b8p8uf.projectCode { rounding := .up } (.finite minusOne)).toNat
-- 0

#eval (b8p8uf.projectCode { saturation := .finite } (.finite minusOne)).toNat
-- 0
```

Last comes the encoder, which finishes the projection with the integer arithmetic of Section 4.7.6. It recomputes the exponent row and trailing field from the rounded dyadic, so it never has to search through the representable codes. The same code path serves a 3-bit descriptor and a 4096-bit one. What we have to prove about it is that it recovers the projected datum and keeps finite results away from reserved codes.

## Constructing and converting P3109 values

Programs use [[FloatLib.Floats.ExecFloat.P3109]], one Lean type per descriptor. It is built on the `Codebook` representation from [chapter 14](#/chapter/fixed-point-logarithmic-codebook-and-block-scaled), and the descriptor's `codebook` says what each code means. `decode_eq_format_decode` identifies its decoder with the descriptor's `decode`, so the same decoding theorems apply. The constructors `zero`, `nan`, `positiveInfinity`, and `negativeInfinity` pick the right codes for the descriptor. For the last two you supply a proof that the requested infinity exists, which `decide` discharges for literal descriptors. `ofFiniteFields?` builds a value from a sign, a biased exponent field and a trailing field, and rejects reserved combinations. `encode?` accepts a datum only if it is representable without rounding, and `ofNatBits` reads a serialized word.

```lean
#eval ExecFloat.P3109.toNatBits (ExecFloat.P3109.nan (format := binary8p4se))
-- 128

#eval (ExecFloat.P3109.ofFiniteFields? (format := binary8p4se) false 15 6).map ExecFloat.P3109.toNatBits
-- some 126

#eval (ExecFloat.P3109.ofFiniteFields? (format := binary8p4se) false 15 7).map ExecFloat.P3109.toNatBits
-- none

#eval (ExecFloat.P3109.ofFiniteFields? (format := b8p8uf) true 0 1).map ExecFloat.P3109.toNatBits
-- none

#eval (binary8p4se.encode? (.finite exact300)).map BitVec.toNat
-- none

#eval (binary8p4se.encode? (.finite { negative := false, significand := 224, exponent := 0 })).map
  BitVec.toNat
-- some 126
```

The field pair $(15, 7)$ is the infinity code of `binary8p4se`, so the checked constructor rejects it, and it also rejects a negative request for an unsigned format. The checked encoder refuses 300, which isn't a datum, but it accepts $224 \cdot 2^0$ even though the decoder writes that value as $14 \cdot 2^4$, because `datumEqual` decides representability by comparing numbers. Projection comes in two forms. `project` and `ofDyadic` take an exact dyadic, while `projectRat` and `ofRat` take an exact rational and round it once from its numerator and denominator, through `roundFiniteRatToPrecision`. Configured binary floats, posits, integers and exact finite arithmetic can all supply rational values, and the rational path never approximates them as dyadics before the final rounding. Both forms take the `mode` and `saturation` options. [Chapter 01](#/chapter/using-the-library)'s shared conversion API uses the rational path. Its `(mode := ...)` option takes a P3109 rounding mode, and `(context := ...)` takes a whole `ProjectionPolicy`. By default you get nearest-even with no saturation request. Let's try, say, three sixteenths and one third.

```lean
abbrev B8P4SE := ExecFloat.P3109 binary8p4se

#eval (ExecFloat.convert (target := B8P4SE) (3 / 16 : Rat)).value?.map
  ExecFloat.P3109.Conversion.decodeRat
-- some (FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite (3 : Rat)/16)

#eval (ExecFloat.convert (target := B8P4SE) (1 / 3 : Rat)).value?.map
  ExecFloat.P3109.Conversion.decodeRat
-- some (FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite (11 : Rat)/32)

#eval (ExecFloat.convert (target := B8P4SE) (1 / 3 : Rat) (mode := .towardZero)).value?.map
  ExecFloat.P3109.Conversion.decodeRat
-- some (FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite (5 : Rat)/16)

#eval ExecFloat.P3109.decode (ExecFloat.P3109.ofRat (format := b8p8uf) (1 / 3))
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := false, significand := 43, exponent := -7 }
```

Three sixteenths is exact, and one third isn't. At that magnitude the quantum is $2^{-5}$, so nearest-even gives $11/32$ and toward zero gives $10/32 = 5/16$. In the unsigned format that behaves like fixed point, one third becomes $43/128$. Conversions also report status flags, and these come from FloatLib's conversion API, since the report defines none. `inexact` means the returned datum differs from the exact input. `overflow` is `roundedExceedsFiniteRange`, which holds when the value rounded to precision in the requested mode lies outside the finite interval, whatever the saturation mode is. So an input that rounds back to an endpoint doesn't overflow. `underflow` is `belowMinPositive`, a nonzero input strictly smaller in magnitude than the least positive datum, which is a narrower condition than IEEE tininess. `saturated` is raised only when saturation changed the rounded result and the returned datum is a finite endpoint.

The record has three more fields, because the conversions in [chapter 01](#/chapter/using-the-library) share it. `wrapped` reports reduction modulo a storage width, which some integer destinations do and no P3109 projection does. `mappedSpecial` is raised when projection changes the class of a special input, for instance when an infinity sent to a finite-domain format becomes NaN or is clamped to an endpoint, and a caller can use the flag to tell that case apart from saturation of a finite input. `invalid` is raised when the source is a signaling NaN. P3109 has a single NaN, so the result is that NaN either way.

```lean
#eval (ExecFloat.convert (target := B8P4SE) (232 : Rat)).status?
-- some { inexact := true,
--   overflow := false,
--   underflow := false,
--   saturated := false,
--   wrapped := false,
--   mappedSpecial := false,
--   invalid := false }

```

For the remaining conversions, only three of the flags above change.

| Exact input | Projection policy | `overflow` | `underflow` | `saturated` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 300 | default | `true` | `false` | `false` |
| 300 | finite | `true` | `false` | `true` |
| $1/3000$ | default | `false` | `true` | `false` |

All three results are inexact, and `wrapped`, `mappedSpecial`, and `invalid` stay false. Three hundred overflows under either policy, but only the finite policy delivers a finite endpoint and reports saturation. One three-thousandth is below the least positive value $2^{-10}$ and reports underflow. And as we saw, 232 rounds back to a finite endpoint, so it doesn't overflow.

## Encoding and rounding theorems

Decode a stored code, encode the resulting datum again, and you should get the original code back. `encodeDatumNat_decode` proves this for the direct encoder at every code of every valid descriptor, and `encode?_decode` says the checked encoder accepts every decoded datum and returns its code. Going the other way, `sameDatum_decode_of_encode?_eq_some` proves that a successful checked encoding decodes to the same datum under `SameDatum`. That relation compares finite values numerically and infinities by sign, and it identifies all NaN payloads, because P3109 has one NaN. `datumEqual_eq_true_iff` ties the executable comparison to the relation.

Projection can change the value, so there we ask for a different equality, namely that the stored result decodes to the datum that rounding and saturation specify. By [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format.roundFiniteToPrecision_fitsPrecisionGrid]], rounding in any of the nine modes lands on the descriptor's grid, `FitsPrecisionGrid`, even when rounding up carries into the next exponent row. `decodePositiveFinite_encodePositiveFinite_eq_of_le` says the direct encoder maps every in-range grid value back to itself. Strict monotonicity supplies the range argument. `encodePositiveFinite_le_maxFiniteBits` shows that a value no larger than `maxFinite` never encodes to a reserved code. The saturation lemmas `sameDatum_decode_encodeFinite` and `sameDatum_decode_encodeSaturate` join these steps into [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format.sameDatum_decode_projectCode]] and its carrier form [[FloatLib.Floats.ExecFloat.P3109.decode_project]]. For the rational path there is one extra grid-fit lemma, `roundFiniteRatToPrecision_fitsPrecisionGrid`, and the rest is reused to get `decode_projectRat`. On top of that, the conversion layer adds [[FloatLib.Floats.ExecFloat.P3109.Conversion.implements_run]], the quantizer contract of [chapter 01](#/chapter/using-the-library), and `sameDatum_decode_run_value`. All of these hold uniformly in $K$, $P$, signedness, domain, and policy. The round-trip theorem below quantifies over every valid descriptor, the projection examples also quantify over the policy, and the last example specializes positive-code monotonicity to the format used above.

```lean
example (format : P3109.Format) (code : BitVec format.bitWidth) :
    format.encode? (format.decode code) = some code :=
  format.encode?_decode code

example (format : P3109.Format) (mode : P3109.RoundingMode) (saturation : P3109.SaturationMode)
    (value : NumericalValue Numerics.Dyadic) :
    P3109.Format.SameDatum
      (ExecFloat.P3109.decode
        (ExecFloat.P3109.project (format := format) value (mode := mode) (saturation := saturation)))
      (format.projectValue { rounding := mode, saturation } value) :=
  ExecFloat.P3109.decode_project value mode saturation

example (format : P3109.Format) (mode : P3109.RoundingMode) (saturation : P3109.SaturationMode)
    (value : NumericalValue Rat) :
    P3109.Format.SameDatum
      (ExecFloat.P3109.decode
        (ExecFloat.P3109.projectRat (format := format) value (mode := mode)
          (saturation := saturation)))
      (format.projectRatValue { rounding := mode, saturation } value) :=
  ExecFloat.P3109.decode_projectRat value mode saturation

example : StrictMono (fun bits => (binary8p4se.decodePositiveFinite bits).toRat) :=
  binary8p4se.decodePositiveFinite_strictMono
```

Proving that the encoder preserves the computed datum says nothing about which datum was computed. For that, the [direction and error proofs](https://github.com/lean-dojo/FloatLib/blob/main/FloatLib/Floats/Formats/P3109/Projection/Direction.lean) show which grid point the rounding step chooses, before saturation, in each of the six deterministic modes of the dyadic kernel. In every case the result is an integer multiple of $2^Q$, by `roundFiniteToPrecision_toRat_eq_int_mul`. None of the three directed modes crosses the input. Each lands within one quantum of it and picks the nearest multiple of the quantum on its side. For truncation that's `roundFiniteToPrecision_towardZero` and `roundFiniteToPrecision_towardZero_maximal`, and for the other two it's `roundFiniteToPrecision_up` and `roundFiniteToPrecision_down` with their `_minimal` and `_maximal` companions. Both nearest modes stay within half a quantum, by `roundFiniteToPrecision_nearestAway_abs_sub_le` and [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format.roundFiniteToPrecision_nearest_abs_sub_le]]. Their tie theorems, `roundFiniteToPrecision_nearestAway_tie` and `roundFiniteToPrecision_nearest_tie`, pick the larger magnitude or the candidate whose P3109 code is even. The second one includes the $P = 1$ rule, and `roundFiniteToPrecision_nearest_tie_even` specializes it to an even significand when $P > 1$. Round-to-odd stays within one quantum, by `roundFiniteToPrecision_toOdd_abs_sub_lt`, and when $P > 1$ it gives an inexact result an odd significand, by `roundFiniteToPrecision_toOdd_odd`. The truncation and nearest-even bounds hold for every valid descriptor and dyadic input, while the parity theorem needs precision greater than one and an inexact result.

```lean
example (format : P3109.Format) (value : Numerics.Dyadic) :
    |(format.roundFiniteToPrecision .towardZero value).toRat| ≤ |value.toRat| ∧
      |value.toRat| <
        |(format.roundFiniteToPrecision .towardZero value).toRat| +
          (2 : Rat) ^ format.quantumExponent value :=
  format.roundFiniteToPrecision_towardZero value

example (format : P3109.Format) (value : Numerics.Dyadic) :
    |(format.roundFiniteToPrecision .nearest value).toRat - value.toRat| ≤
      (2 : Rat) ^ format.quantumExponent value / 2 :=
  format.roundFiniteToPrecision_nearest_abs_sub_le value

example (format : P3109.Format) (value : Numerics.Dyadic) (hprecision : 1 < format.precision)
    (hinexact : (format.roundFiniteToPrecision .toOdd value).toRat ≠ value.toRat) :
    (format.roundFiniteToPrecision .toOdd value).significand % 2 = 1 :=
  format.roundFiniteToPrecision_toOdd_odd value hprecision hinexact
```

The random bits are inputs too. [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.P3109.Format.roundFiniteToPrecision_report_spec]] proves that the shift-based dyadic kernel follows the report's formula for every supplied word, so once you fix that word, the theorem tells you which grid point comes back. On the rational arithmetic path we prove that formula for all nine modes, including code parity and exact ties. External precision rounding uses the destination's actual precision and bias, and the encoding proofs preserve the rounded and saturated datum. Finally, the conversion theorem [[FloatLib.Floats.ExecFloat.P3109.Conversion.status_overflow_implies_outside]] proves that a raised overflow flag means the exact input lies outside the destination's finite range. For small descriptors, such as $3 \le K \le 10$, testing every code is practical, and our [independent comparison](#/chapter/external-validation) uses the working group's value tables to test the bit layout. That tests our reading of the report, while the theorems above establish the rounding formulas.

## Arithmetic rounds the whole expression

`ExecFloat.P3109.add`, `sub`, `mul`, `div`, and `fma` work on values of one format. They project into `(target := ...)`, which defaults to that same format, under the `mode` and `saturation` options, and `unary`, `binary`, and `ternary` accept operands of different formats. A literal such as `(1.5 : ExecFloat.P3109 .binary8p4se)` rounds once with the default policy. `==` is numerical equality, so NaN is unequal to itself :) Finite inputs decode to exact rationals, and addition, subtraction, multiplication and division form the exact result before calling the destination's rational projection. Division needs a nonzero denominator for the finite quotient rule. `square` gives the same interface for $x^2$, and `square_eq_mul` proves it equal to multiplication for every target and policy, including the supplied random word in a stochastic mode. A fused multiply-add forms $xy+z$ before any rounding or saturation. In `binary8p4se`, the largest finite value is 224, and all three operands of

$$
16 \cdot 16 - 224 = 32
$$

are representable, and so is the final result. With nearest-even rounding and no saturation request, a separate multiplication gives positive infinity, because 256 exceeds the finite range, and subtracting 224 afterwards leaves infinity. With finite saturation the separate multiplication clamps to 224 instead, and the subtraction gives zero. `fma` returns 32 under either policy, because it projects only after the subtraction. For arbitrary finite operands, the [arithmetic theorem](https://github.com/lean-dojo/FloatLib/blob/main/FloatLib/Floats/Formats/P3109/Arithmetic/Proof.lean) `decode_fma_finite` states this contract. The decoded output is the same datum as `target.projectRatValue { rounding := mode, saturation } (.finite (x * y + z))`. More generally, `decode_binary` and `decode_ternary` cover the executable operations and their exceptional cases, which follow the P3109 model. Opposite infinities in addition, zero times infinity, and every division by zero produce the single NaN, and none of them carries IEEE signaling metadata or status flags.

### Square-root rounding by exact comparisons

You can round a square root by comparing squares of rational boundaries, and it's easy to do one by hand. In `binary8p4se` the candidates around $\sqrt{2}$ are $11/8$ and $12/8$, with midpoint $23/16$. To decide which is closer to $\sqrt{2}$, compare

$$
2 = \frac{512}{256} < \left(\frac{23}{16}\right)^2 = \frac{529}{256}.
$$

So the lower candidate is closer, and nearest-even square root returns $11/8$. In the [square-root kernel](https://github.com/lean-dojo/FloatLib/blob/main/FloatLib/Floats/Formats/P3109/Arithmetic/Sqrt/Runtime.lean) every decision is made this way, with squared rational boundaries. It implements `sqrt`, reciprocal square root `rsqrt`, and `hypot`, which forms $x^2+y^2$ exactly before taking its square root. Rounding to precision still comes before saturation.

`roundSqrtRatToPrecision_eq_real` proves agreement with the reference rounder applied to the exact real square root. The configured theorems `decode_sqrt`, `decode_rsqrt`, and `decode_hypot` carry that agreement through encoding for all nine rounding modes, including the stochastic modes with their supplied random word. A negative square-root input gives NaN, and so does a nonpositive reciprocal-square-root input. For stochastic rounding, `sqrtRoundAway_stochasticA`, `sqrtRoundAway_stochasticB`, and `sqrtRoundAway_stochasticC` connect the squared-threshold tests to the report's floor and nearest-integer formulas. They use the fractional part of the exact real square root and cover every supplied random word, so no approximate square root hides in that step. The annoying part is that exact rational intermediates can still get large in formats with a wide exponent range.

## Mixed formats and external destinations

Section 4.5 of the report [@p3109InterimReport] names the minimum operation specializations through three sets of formats. FloatLib supplies them, and for the external set it takes all three formats the report allows.

| Set | Formats |
| --- | --- |
| `F4` | Binary4p2sf |
| `F8` | Binary8p4se and Binary8p3se |
| `FX` | binary16, binary32, and BFloat16 |

In the [mixed-format API](https://github.com/lean-dojo/FloatLib/tree/main/FloatLib/Floats/Formats/P3109/Arithmetic/Mixed) each operand can have its own type. Exact decoders map finite inputs to rationals, and a `Destination` argument picks the final projection. That covers conversions and reciprocals among these formats, mixed addition, subtraction, multiplication and division, and fused multiply-add and add-add. Section 4.5's required specializations use nearest-even with no saturation request, and the `mode` and `saturation` options of every mixed operation select the other report policies. Fused add-add, `Mixed.faa`, keeps all three terms until it knows the destination. Say we take the `binary8p4se` inputs 16 and 1 and a binary32 accumulator of $-16$. The exact expression is

$$
(16+1)+(-16)=1.
$$

If you rounded the first sum into `binary8p4se`, you'd lose the one. The sum 17 is our tie between 16 and 18 from earlier, and nearest-even picks 16, which would make the final result zero. `Mixed.faa` sends the exact sum 1 straight to the binary32 destination, and `faa_eq_project_finite` states that contract, just as `fma_eq_project_finite` does for the fused product and addend. An external destination uses its own precision, bias and finite endpoints, so binary16 keeps bias 15 and doesn't adopt a P3109 bias of 16. In the [external-destination theorem](https://github.com/lean-dojo/FloatLib/blob/main/FloatLib/Floats/Formats/P3109/Arithmetic/External/Proof.lean), `project_refines`, we prove that encoding the rounded and saturated datum preserves its finite value, its infinity sign, or its prescribed exceptional representation. So the encoder hides no second numerical rounding. For the exceptional representation we follow the report. An external negative zero enters the calculation as the single zero, and a zero result is written as external positive zero. Input NaN diagnostics collapse to the report's one NaN, and an external NaN result uses the canonical quiet encoding. These adapters are destinations you choose for P3109 operations, and ordinary binary arithmetic keeps its IEEE signed-zero and NaN rules.

### Scaling before arithmetic

The minimum scaled operations use Binary8p1uf scales with `F4` or `F8` elements and an `F8` or external result. `Mixed.scaledAdd`, `scaledSub`, and `scaledMul` form $sx+ty$, $sx-ty$, and $(sx)(ty)$ without rounding and then project once, with an implicit result scale of one. Neither multiplication by an input scale rounds before the main operation. `scaledAdd_eq_project_finite` and its subtraction and multiplication counterparts state those exact expressions. Exceptional values still follow closed arithmetic, so a zero scale times an infinite element gives NaN, and a NaN scale propagates even when its element is zero. If you treated scaling as a bit shift that skips those cases, you'd be computing a different operation.

## Comparisons, extrema, and neighboring values

Comparisons are unordered at NaN, and that includes NaN's equality with itself. Extrema differ in their ordering and their treatment of special operands. `minimumMagnitude (-2) 2` chooses $-2$, while `maximumMagnitude (-2) 2` chooses $2$, because equal magnitudes are settled by numerical order. `minimumNumber` can return a numerical operand when the other is NaN, but `minimum` propagates NaN, and `minimumFinite` and `maximumFinite` prefer a finite operand even over an infinity. We prove all of these cases in the [extrema proofs](https://github.com/lean-dojo/FloatLib/blob/main/FloatLib/Floats/Formats/P3109/Arithmetic/Extrema/Proof.lean).

Value and format queries expose the classification and the grid parameters. Value queries classify zero, one, NaN, infinity, finite values, negative signs, normals and subnormals, and format queries give widths, precision, signedness, domain, bias and the distinguished finite values. Precision one is a good test of what a query means. There are no nonzero subnormals, so `maxSubnormalOf` returns NaN, as `maxSubnormalOf_eq_nan` proves.

The neighbor operations step through the representable values. `nextGreaterThan` and `nextLessThan` ask for a strictly greater or lesser datum. In `binary8p4se` the next datum above 224 is positive infinity, and the next one above positive infinity is NaN, because no greater datum exists :) IEEE `nextUp` would leave positive infinity where it is. The neighbor implementation works on the codes themselves, and the proofs connect consecutive finite codes to numerical order and rule out a representable value in between. Every operation listed here has decoding, projection, ordering and classification proofs, but they don't show compliance with every requirement of the interim report.

## How P3109 differs from the OCP formats

The formats of [chapter 11](#/chapter/low-precision-formats-for-machine-learning) overlap with P3109 in their FNUZ variants, and the comparison starts with the exponent bias. An IEEE-style layout with $w_e$ exponent bits has bias $2^{w_e - 1} - 1$, and a P3109 layout with the same field has bias $2^{w_e - 1}$. E4M3FN has bias 7 and a largest finite value of $448 = 1.75 \cdot 2^8$. With $K = 8$ and $P = 4$, the signed P3109 format has bias 8 and a largest finite value of 224 with infinities, or 240 without. Every nonzero finite E4M3FN code denotes exactly twice the P3109 value of the same code. The reserved codes disagree too. E4M3FN puts NaN at `0x7f` and `0xff` and keeps negative zero at `0x80`, while P3109 puts NaN at `0x80` and, in the extended domain, infinities at `0x7f` and `0xff`. The ONNX FNUZ formats went the other way. E4M3FNUZ has bias 8, a single zero, no infinity, and its NaN at `0x80`, the negative-zero word. Those are the same decisions a signed finite P3109 descriptor makes, so let's check that over the whole code space.

```lean
open FloatLib.Floats.Formats.BinaryInterchange

/-- Whether a P3109 descriptor and a binary `FloatFormat` give every eight-bit code the same meaning. -/
def sameByteValues (format : P3109.Format) (fmt : FloatFormat) : Bool :=
  (List.range 256).all fun code =>
    let word : Model fmt := Model.ofNatBits code
    match format.decode (BitVec.ofNat format.bitWidth code), Model.toDyadic? word with
    | .finite d, some m => d.toRat == m.toRat
    | .exceptional _, none => Model.isNaN word
    | _, _ => false

#eval sameByteValues b8p4sf FloatFormat.e4m3fnuz
-- true

#eval sameByteValues (.signed 8 3 .finite) FloatFormat.e5m2fnuz
-- true

#eval sameByteValues binary8p4se FloatFormat.e4m3fn
-- false

#eval sameByteValues binary8p3se FloatFormat.e5m2
-- false
```

So [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.BinaryInterchange.FloatFormat.e4m3fnuz]] and the signed finite descriptor with $K = 8$, $P = 4$ agree on all 256 codes, and [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.BinaryInterchange.FloatFormat.e5m2fnuz]] agrees with $K = 8$, $P = 3$ the same way. This comes from evaluating every code, so it isn't a theorem, and all it says is that the value sets coincide. Whether two implementations agree on arithmetic is another question. [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.BinaryInterchange.FloatFormat.e4m3fn]] and [[FloatLib.Floats.Formats.BinaryInterchange.FloatFormat.e5m2]] aren't P3109 formats under any descriptor. Their bias is one less, and E5M2 spends the eight codes of its all-ones exponent on two infinities and six NaNs, where P3109 spends three codes on one NaN and two infinities. Both keep a negative zero that P3109 doesn't have.

```lean
#eval (FloatFormat.e4m3fn.exponentBias, binary8p4se.exponentBias)
-- (7, 8)

#eval Model.toDyadic? (Model.ofNatBits (fmt := FloatFormat.e4m3fn) 0x7e)
-- some { negative := false, significand := 14, exponent := 5 }

#eval binary8p4se.decode 0x7e
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := false, significand := 14, exponent := 4 }

#eval Model.isInf (Model.ofNatBits (fmt := FloatFormat.e5m2) 0x7c)
-- true

#eval binary8p3se.decode 0x7c
-- FloatLib.Numerics.NumericalValue.finite { negative := false, significand := 4, exponent := 13 }
```

Byte `0x7e` is 448 in E4M3FN and 224 in P3109. Byte `0x7c` is positive infinity in E5M2 and $2^{15}$ in the corresponding P3109 format, which keeps its infinity three codes higher, at `0x7f`. Agreeing with FNUZ code by code covers only part of the P3109 family, though. `FloatFormat` doesn't admit unsigned formats with no sign bit, $P = 1$ with no trailing bits, or $P = K$ with one exponent bit. P3109 also classifies its codes differently, with a midpoint NaN, endpoint infinities and mirrored negative codes, and its own decoder handles those rules without adding cases to the binary-interchange theorems. The operation sets differ too. On the binary-interchange side you get IEEE arithmetic and status flags, with real-number proofs that assume the IEEE reading of the reserved patterns. P3109 arithmetic evaluates the closed expression and then applies its rounding and saturation policy, even when the destination is a binary format. So matching a value table doesn't make the two APIs interchangeable, because their arithmetic still differs in its zero, NaN, saturation, and neighbour rules.
